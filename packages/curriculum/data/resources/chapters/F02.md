## F02-L01 — Linear algebra: vectors, matrices and dot products

### Core concept

A vector is an ordered set of coordinates; matrix multiplication composes linear transformations. Dot products combine magnitude and alignment, while cosine similarity normalizes by vector norms.

### Theory and reasoning

A dot product sums pairwise products and requires equal vector lengths. For X with n rows and d features, w of length d and scalar b, Xw+b yields n scores. Matrix multiplication is not elementwise multiplication; shape reasoning prevents accidental broadcasting.

### Worked scenario

Calculate scores Xw+b for a batch with shape (n,d) and weights (d,).

### Design and verification

Elementwise X*w returns a matrix, not scores. Sum along feature axis or use @, and check dimensions.

### Practice

Compute batch scores by hand for two rows and verify with NumPy.

Acceptance: Scores match and output shape is (2,).

## F02-L02 — Eigenvalues, SVD and projections

### Core concept

Eigenvectors preserve direction under a square linear transform; singular value decomposition factors a rectangular matrix into orthogonal directions and singular values. PCA projects centered data onto directions with greatest variance.

### Theory and reasoning

SVD writes X=UΣVᵀ with orthonormal singular vectors and nonnegative singular values. The best rank-k approximation under Frobenius norm retains the top k singular components; its squared error equals the sum of discarded squared singular values. Eigenvectors of XᵀX are right singular vectors in real-valued cases.

### Worked scenario

Approximate a small matrix using its largest singular value and compare reconstruction errors.

### Design and verification

Applying PCA to uncentered data measures variation around the origin; center using train statistics when variance around the mean is the goal.

### Practice

Reconstruct a matrix with ranks 1 and 2; calculate Frobenius error.

Acceptance: Rank-2 error is no larger than rank-1 error.

## F02-L03 — Probability, distributions and Bayes rule

### Core concept

Conditional probability measures an event given observed information. Bayes rule updates a prior with a likelihood and normalizes by evidence. Bernoulli, binomial and normal distributions model different types of random variables.

### Theory and reasoning

Conditional probability reverses direction only through Bayes' rule: P(D given +)=P(+ given D)P(D)/P(+). At low prevalence false positives may dominate even with a good test. Independence and conditional independence are distinct assumptions.

### Worked scenario

A rare condition has a positive test; combine sensitivity, specificity and prevalence to calculate posterior probability.

### Design and verification

Confusing sensitivity with positive predictive value gives 90%. Draw a frequency table and compute the denominator across both classes.

### Practice

Use prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90% and specificity 95% to calculate posterior.

Acceptance: Posterior is about 15.4%, not 90%.

## F02-L04 — Statistics, sampling and confidence intervals

### Core concept

A statistic summarizes a sample; sampling variability describes how it changes across samples. Bootstrap resampling approximates estimator uncertainty under appropriate sampling assumptions. A frequentist confidence interval has coverage under repeated sampling, not a posterior probability for one fixed interval.

### Theory and reasoning

A bootstrap samples n observations with replacement to approximate the sampling distribution of a statistic. Standard error for independent sample means falls approximately as 1/√n. Percentile intervals do not automatically fix sampling bias or dependence.

### Worked scenario

Bootstrap a mean from independent observations and estimate a percentile interval.

### Design and verification

Resampling individual time points breaks autocorrelation; use appropriate blocks or model-based intervals, and distinguish sample size from resample count.

### Practice

Generate bootstrap intervals for sample sizes 20 and 200 from the same distribution.

Acceptance: Explain why larger samples generally yield narrower intervals.

## F02-L05 — Calculus, gradients and optimization

### Core concept

A derivative measures local sensitivity; gradients collect partial derivatives. Gradient descent subtracts a learning-rate-scaled gradient from parameters. The chain rule propagates derivatives through composed operations.

### Theory and reasoning

For f(x)=x², derivative is 2x and gradient descent gives x_next=(1−2η)x. Absolute multiplier below one implies convergence; a negative multiplier oscillates, and magnitude above one diverges. This simple quadratic illustrates why learning rate depends on curvature.

### Worked scenario

Minimize (w-3)^2 using updates w = w - lr*2*(w-3).

### Design and verification

Looking only at loss hides sign oscillation. Plot parameters and loss together and check gradient magnitude.

### Practice

Compare trajectories for learning rates 0.1, 0.9 and 1.1.

Acceptance: Identify convergence, oscillation and divergence using loss values.

## F02-L06 — Information theory and numerical stability

### Core concept

Entropy measures uncertainty of a distribution; cross entropy penalizes predicted probabilities under a target distribution. KL divergence measures distribution mismatch and is asymmetric. Log-sum-exp stabilizes exponential calculations.

### Theory and reasoning

For a true class, negative log likelihood is −log p. Cross entropy averages this loss; KL divergence adds a distribution-dependent constant relative to cross entropy. Stable softmax subtracts max logit before exponentiation; log-sum-exp avoids overflow.

### Worked scenario

Compare cross entropy for correct-class probabilities 0.9 and 0.1.

### Design and verification

Exponentiating large logits returns inf; avoid probabilities rounded to zero and use logits-based losses where possible.

### Practice

Compute negative log likelihood for both probabilities and explain the penalty.

Acceptance: The 0.1 prediction has the higher loss; use stable log handling.
